## Already priced in or not

**Bottom line:**
*   Earnings surprise drift and momentum strategies show the strongest evidence of underreaction, with returns often persisting for weeks or months after the news.
*   After accounting for trading costs, profits remain viable in small and low coverage stocks but shrink significantly in large, liquid markets.
*   Holding periods typically range from 21 days to 6 months, depending on the specific event and market conditions.

### The methods with the best evidence

The most robust finding in this field is Post Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD). This occurs when prices adjust slowly to earnings news rather than moving all at once. The table shows that a strategy buying stocks with the highest earnings surprises and selling those with the lowest can generate approximately 4.2% abnormal return over 60 days [P5]. A broader review of over 215 papers confirms positive drift of around 2% over 60 trading days, which amounts to 18% annualized excess returns [P8]. This effect is strongest in small, illiquid stocks where information diffuses more slowly [P8, P67].

Momentum strategies also rely on gradual information diffusion. A standard approach uses 6-month past returns to predict the next 6 months. This method yields 0.853% per month across all stocks [P5783]. The effect is even stronger in stocks with low analyst coverage, where the spread between top and bottom deciles reaches 0.824% per month [P5783]. Another study finds a spread of 15.4% over the first year based on past 6-month returns [P5815].

Distraction and attention limits drive significant underreaction. When earnings are announced on a Friday, the immediate price response is 15% lower and the delayed drift is 70% higher compared to other days [P81]. During high distraction days with many competing announcements, the drift strategy generates an alpha of 1.64% per month [P82]. Visual salience also matters. Front page articles see 176% larger absolute excess returns in the first 10 minutes, incorporating information within one hour, while non front page news takes more than two days [P3000].

Analyst forecast revisions provide another signal. The CAF hedge strategy, which buys stocks with the highest forecast revisions versus the lowest, generates 9.3% cumulative raw return over 12 months [P822]. This effect is driven by the smallest quintile of stocks, which shows 1.42% per month [P822]. Additionally, news diffuses through supply chains. Supplier stock prices underreact to customer news by roughly 40%, yielding abnormal returns of 1.45% per month [P83].

### Where it is weak or disagrees

Despite strong historical results, the evidence has limitations. The magnitude of PEAD has declined in recent years. One study finds that 23.2% of market return on announcement day is delayed for extreme surprises, but this declined from 1.56% in 1998-2002 to 0.61% in 2018-2022 [P2919]. Transaction costs often eat profits in small caps, making some strategies unviable after fees [P3105, P62]. Some papers contradict the drift narrative. One study finds most information is reflected in prices the day it is released, with anomaly returns of only 5.62 basis points on news days [P3472].

Several papers report findings that support the theory of underreaction but do not provide a tradable strategy return. These studies offer statistical evidence rather than profit numbers. For example, one paper finds the sum of four weekly lag coefficients is 0.2304 for big firms predicting small firms, but it reports no strategy return [P117]. The effect comes mostly after bad news: small firms' response to big firm returns is 0.2841 after negative weeks and 0.1283 after positive weeks, and it sits in small stocks at a weekly horizon [P117]. Another finds price elasticity to net asset value is 0.50, indicating underreaction, but reports no strategy return [P5981]. Research on Friday stock swap announcements notes the market reaction is muted, yet it reports no strategy return [P2893].

Other studies confirm underreaction qualitatively without quantifying profits. One paper states long run abnormal returns are consistent with underreaction, but specific return numbers are not reported in the abstract [P6503]. Another finds markets ignore schedule changes until announcement, but reports no strategy return [P2916]. Evidence of informed trading before announcements exists, but this paper reports no strategy return [P3419]. Price reactions vary over time according to one study, but it reports no strategy return [P3233]. Underreaction is stronger for small firms and 10-K filings, yet this paper reports no strategy return [P3223]. One study finds underreaction to earnings but overreaction to product news, and it reports no strategy return [P3094]. Finally, research shows high analyst firms lead low analyst firms, but it reports no strategy return [P5826].

Pre announcement moves have also decayed. Pre announcement returns peaked at 0.180% on day t-1 in the 1990s but decayed to 0.002% by 2012 [P2884]. Pre merger runup declined from 5.12% in 2002-2009 to 2.84% in 2009-2011 [P3415].

### Numbers worth remembering

*   **4.2% abnormal return over 60 days** for the highest versus lowest earnings surprise deciles (US, 1974-1986) [P5].
*   **9.3% cumulative raw return** for the CAF hedge portfolio over 12 months (US, 1983-2005) [P822].
*   **1.64% alpha per month** for the distraction strategy hedge portfolio (US, 1995-2004) [P82].
*   **23.2% of market return** on announcement day is delayed for extreme surprises over 3 days (US, 1998-2022) [P2919].
*   **2.89% 30-day alpha** net of 1.66% spread for bad news (US, 1995-2004) [P3105].

### What this means for a trader

Traders should expect delayed adjustment for earnings surprises, especially in small stocks. You should focus on stocks with low analyst coverage where information diffusion is slower. Be aware that Friday announcements often lead to larger drifts because investors are distracted. Do not chase immediate spikes if the news is highly salient, as this can signal overreaction and reversal. Pre announcement price moves have become less reliable in recent years. Always account for transaction costs, as they can eliminate profits in illiquid stocks.

### What this means for the app

The app should flag stocks with low analyst coverage as having higher potential for delayed price reaction. Alert users to earnings on Fridays or high distraction days with a 'high drift potential' storyline. Map customer supplier relationships to predict supplier drift based on customer news. Track 'Expected Report Date' versus 'Actual Date' to warn of gradual declines when reports are late. Flag salient headlines as potential reversal signals rather than continuation signals. Monitor 'hard to borrow' status with earnings news to adjust expected reaction speed. For papers that report no strategy return, use the signals qualitatively. For example, use schedule changes or analyst coverage levels as context rather than quantitative return drivers.
