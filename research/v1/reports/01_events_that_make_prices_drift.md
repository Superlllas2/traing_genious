## Events that make prices drift

**Bottom line:** Post earnings announcement drift and analyst upgrades offer the strongest evidence for price continuation over weeks to months. Returns after costs are often reduced but remain positive for small stocks and specific insider signals. Holding periods typically range from one month to one year depending on the event type.

### The methods with the best evidence

The most reliable method for capturing drift is trading on earnings surprises. Post earnings announcement drift occurs when prices continue to move in the direction of the surprise for weeks after the report. A study of hedge portfolios based on analyst consensus earnings surprises shows a 12-month size adjusted hedge return of 13.70% [P9]. After accounting for transaction costs, profits remain between 2.80% and 5.76% depending on the cost measure [P9]. Another study found a 4.2% abnormal return over 60 days following surprises [P5]. This effect is stronger in small firms where returns reach 5.3% compared to 2.8% for large firms [P5].

Analyst recommendation changes also drive drift. Buying on analyst upgrades yields an average return of 1.02% over two days after controlling for transaction costs [P334]. This evidence is supported by effective bid ask spreads for Nasdaq stocks [P334]. Upgrades based on earnings estimates show a 3.11% monthly alpha for earnings based upgrades [P354]. However, the magnitude of this drift varies by market conditions. Downgrades show a cumulative abnormal return of -2.678% in crises versus -1.687% in good times [P5883].

Insider trading provides another signal. Opportunistic insider buys predict drift with 82 basis points monthly long short abnormal returns [P7238]. This signal is distinct from routine trading and requires classification to filter noise. Share repurchases also indicate future outperformance. Post completion repurchase alphas are 0.6682% monthly over 12 months [P488]. Opaque firms experience positive abnormal returns twice the magnitude of transparent firms around repurchases [P495].

### Where it is weak or disagrees

Evidence weakens in recent years and for large stocks. One study confirms PEAD disappeared for large stocks post-2006 [P15]. Microcap drift lasts only five days in this later period [P15]. Transaction costs frequently erase profits. Costs consume 70 to 100% of gross PEAD profits in some samples [P2]. Another study notes transaction costs eliminate net returns for analyst strategies [P337]. Shorting constraints limit many strategies. Shorting is required for many strategies such as shorting demand outward shifts [P5854]. This limits retail access to certain drift signals.

Some papers report no strategy return despite finding statistical significance. P93 states analyst underreaction is at most half the size of drift [P93]. P102 finds celebrity analysts adjust faster but provides no return percentages [P102]. P117 reports no strategy return, only a coefficient of 0.2304 for lead lag effects [P117]. The effect comes mostly after bad news: small firms' response to big firm returns is 0.2841 after negative weeks and 0.1283 after positive weeks, and it sits in small stocks at a weekly horizon [P117]. P1 links drift to idiosyncratic volatility without a strategy return [P1]. P10 notes small traders react to random walk surprises but specific returns are not reported [P10]. P357 notes long term returns following experienced analysts outperform, but specific return percentages are not reported [P357]. P17 finds revenue surprise data but does not state returns [P17]. P495 finds opaque firms experience positive abnormal returns twice the magnitude of transparent firms, but exact returns are not reported [P495]. P355 finds target price revisions trigger stronger responses, though exact magnitudes are not reported [P355]. P643 distinguishes sales where large sales predict negative returns while small sales predict positive returns, though specific percentages are not reported [P643]. P359 finds contrarian buys generate drift, though exact holding periods are not reported [P359]. P31 finds text based drift is larger than classic PEAD but exact numbers are not reported [P31]. P173 finds positive news leads to return continuation, but specific return percentages are not reported [P173]. P704 cites a 2% return for combined surprise models but specific periods are not reported [P704]. P707 finds institutional herding coefficient is 0.018 on surprise [P707]. P829 shows positive returns for upward revisions in Australia, but percentages are not reported [P829]. P26 summarizes the phenomenon without specific return figures [P26].

### What this means for a trader

Traders should prioritize events with high drift magnitude and low liquidity costs. Focus on small, low volatility stocks with earnings surprise streaks. Combine rating levels with changes and filter for experienced analysts. Buy on insider purchases and ignore small sales. Treat index changes as immediate events rather than drift signals. Always warn users that historical returns may not persist after costs or in modern markets. Shorting constraints limit access to some signals.

### What this means for the app

The app should filter signals by liquidity and transaction costs. Flag PEAD events for 4 to 6 week holds. Filter analyst upgrades by earnings estimate revisions. Warn users on shorting constraints. Add insider classification to filter routine versus opportunistic trades. Monitor industry leaders for follower drift. Classify news as identified event versus noise. Flag contrarian buy side signals. The app must link events into storylines to confirm the drift.
